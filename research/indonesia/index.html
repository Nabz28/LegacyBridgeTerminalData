What survives

Indonesia is not in trouble the way we first said it was. Real GDP grew 5.6% in Q1, the fastest since 2022. Manufacturing is running above last year. Bank asset quality across the system is the best it has been in twenty-three years. Our first draft called this a broad economic contraction. It isn't one.

Three things underneath are genuinely bad, and they're specific.

Real wages in the six provinces where most Indonesians work have not moved in eight years. The index sits at 100.13 against 100 in February 2018, and 4.18% below its 2020 peak. Almost all the new jobs are going to the educated third of the workforce. The 53% with junior-high schooling or less saw employment grow 0.23%.

That same group is defaulting on its mortgages. Apartment arrears are 4.16%, against a 2014–17 peak of 2.62%. Small landed houses are at 6.42% against 3.62%. Both are the worst in twelve years of data. The expensive end of the market is fine, and actually below its own pre-2018 peak.

And money got expensive in a hurry. Bank Indonesia went from cutting to hiking on 20 May. Overnight bank funding went from 3.66% in January to 5.98% by 10 June. Banks had set their lending rates for a cutting cycle that ended seven weeks later.

So the story isn't a slowdown. It's a growing economy where the bottom half has been squeezed for eight years and has now started to default, while the cost of money has jumped.

What we're confident about

  1. Bank Indonesia reversed course on 20 May and one of our own data files still shows the old rate. The deposit facility went 3.75% to 4.25% on 20 May, then 4.50% on 9 June. Policy rate 5.25%. We checked this against the source ourselves: both dates and both levels are exact. The banking file in our archive still reads 5.50/3.75 and stops at 30 April. If you query that file you'll get the wrong answer.
  2. Banks are getting squeezed on margin, not on volume. Overnight funding costs 5.98%. Working capital lending was priced at 7.96% on 1 April, the cheapest in the whole window. That's a 198bp gap, down from 421bp in January. Meanwhile loans are growing 9.4% and deposits 9.5%, so this is not a credit crunch. Any margin forecast built off April lending rates is already wrong.
  3. Only households are being rationed. Corporate and investment lending grew 10.8%. Consumer lending grew 6.1% and has slowed every month since February 2025. Consumer loans are now 27.6% of the book, the lowest in the window. Banks charge households 2.57pp more than corporates, the widest gap on record. They're running a price war for corporate business and charging households for it.
  4. The consumer bad-debt problem is real but small, and the headline hides it. Consumer non-performing loans grew 19.4% while consumer lending grew 6.1%. That pushed the consumer default rate from 2.02% to 2.28%. The system-wide rate is 2.12%, which is close to the best in twenty-three years of data. So the aggregate looks healthy because it is healthy. The problem is one slice of it.
  5. The mortgage market has split by price point, and the cheap end is at twelve-year highs. This is our strongest finding and it survived two separate attempts to break it. Checking the full database back to 2014, three mass-market segments are at all-time highs: apartments at 4.16% against a 2014–17 worst of 2.62%, mid-size apartments at 4.79% against 2.95%, and small landed houses at 6.42% against 3.62%. Each set its record last month. Expensive landed houses are at 3.06%, below their own pre-2018 worst. One caveat: the very smallest apartments, at 6.39%, are not a record. They hit 10.2% in July 2018.
  6. One Indonesian stock has a price nobody could actually trade at. DCI Indonesia is 44.9% of the listed tech sector, trades at 476 times earnings, and turns over IDR 195mn a day. At that rate it would take 2.36 million trading days to sell the company. We originally listed four such names. Three of them don't hold up: PANI is the most liquid property stock on the exchange, Bayan doesn't clear 40% of anything, and PGUN was 17.4% of its sector, not the 42.7% we published. That last error came from our own data extract.
  7. The Fed restarted buying bonds in December, and our explanation of why was wrong. The facts check out exactly. Quantitative tightening ended in December with the portfolio at $6,259bn. The Fed's Treasury bill holdings had been frozen at $195bn since April 2024, then went to $212bn, $254bn, $309bn, $366bn, $413bn. Bills did all of the buying. But we said reserves fell because the reverse repo facility drained. They fell because the Treasury rebuilt $401bn of cash between August and November. The repo facility draining $54bn was the only thing softening the blow. In 2023 the same kind of Treasury build was absorbed entirely by that facility and reserves actually went up. By last August the cushion was nearly gone.
  8. US payrolls are stalling, but not the way we described. Payrolls grew 0.16% over the year. Of the 902,000 jobs announced across twelve monthly reports, only 260,000 showed up in the level. The other 642,000 were revised away. That part holds. But we said stripping out healthcare leaves the US shedding 400,000 jobs a year, and that's misleading: 330,000 of it is a federal government headcount cut. Private jobs outside healthcare are down 161,000, or 0.15%. Flat, not shrinking.

What this means for you

The Indonesian case isn't that the economy is weak. It's that a growing economy has left half its workforce behind for eight years, that group has started defaulting, and the price of money just jumped. The real-economy data we have runs to April. The rate move happened in May and June. Nothing in the industry numbers has felt it yet.

Taken together these do not just trim report two, they relocate its argument. The claims that failed were the ones asserting market distress: heavy-volume distribution, a bond market pricing stress, a manufacturing sector rolling over, businesses refusing to hire. On the data, none of those hold. Indonesian domestic demand improved year on year, the manufacturing index is above last year and turning up, the ten-year is flat, system asset quality is at a twenty-three-year best, and the equity market is falling on volume 35% below January's pace.

What survived is narrower and almost entirely about funding and the currency: the 20 May policy reversal and a further 25bp after it, overnight funding up 233bp, deposits leaking into cash and foreign currency, the banks' marginal spread halved, the rupiah weaker against the yuan and the euro than against the dollar with effective rates at sample lows, and a consumer credit tail , mass-segment mortgages and consumption NPLs, deteriorating inside an aggregate that is otherwise healthy.

That is a better thesis than the one it replaces, and it changes what to watch. A demand collapse would show up in activity data; a funding squeeze shows up in the currency, the deposit mix and the interbank rate. Report two pointed at the wrong instruments.

Round four checked the eight claims that had never been verified. Seven failed and one needed qualification, and two of the eight were themselves corrections this programme had already made. On the US energy pass-through and the Chinese consumer we have now been wrong in both directions on the same question.

That is the most useful thing in this report. A correction is not self-validating: it is a new claim, made under the same pressures as the one it replaces, and in this programme corrections have failed at roughly the same rate as originals. The US fiscal note is the sharpest illustration , it correctly exposed an accounting artifact in the deficit data and then committed the same error on the financing side, quoting a marketable-debt figure that is itself a debt-ceiling artifact.

The practical rule that follows: a corrected claim carries no more assurance than an uncorrected one until it has been independently checked. This report now labels every stored finding with its verification state for exactly that reason, and eleven findings remain unverified, including the Fed balance-sheet claim. The agents assigned to check it hit the session usage limit before completing.

What later rounds added

6 findings that strengthen rather than correct, all from data report two either did not reach or read wrongly.

Rupiah weakness is broad, not a dollar story

Over the twelve months to 1 May the rupiah fell 9.4% against the dollar but 13.8% against the yuan and 10.9% against the euro, and both effective-rate constructions in the archive sit at their 2018–2026 sample lows. This is stronger than what we claimed and it relocates the problem: the pressure is on the currency itself, not on the dollar.

Bank Indonesia tightened further than reported

The policy rate went to 5.25% on 20 May and the corridor tightened a further 25bp after that. Report two captured the first move and not the second.

Reserves are down but not unprecedented

Reserves are 12.4% off peak on the headline FX series and 5.7% on the alternative series in the same file: the third-largest drawdown since 2018, not a record. April's 4.94 months of import cover is Lebaran-distorted; Eid-neutral it is 5.61 months, second-lowest since 2018 but above the 2022 trough.

Coal mining and washing capacity utilisation fell to 66.41% at 31 March from 71.88% a year earlier, the lowest outside the 2020 COVID quarter. This is the most direct read available on the demand side for Indonesian thermal exports and it was never in the report.

US heavy trucks are in a capex recession that freight volume flatly contradicts

Rolling twelve-month heavy truck sales of 395,271 at 31 March are the lowest of the entire 2018–2026 window, below even the COVID trough of 409,508, while the freight transportation services index set a record at 139.8 in February, the highest February on record. Fleets are moving more goods than ever and refusing to replace equipment. Note also that the −8.7% light-vehicle print report two quoted is mostly a base effect against a two-month tariff pre-buy: the twelve-month average is −0.35% and March 2026 sits 3.9% above March 2024.

US residential is a stall with a single-family recession inside it

Total housing starts are flat on a twelve-month average, 1,368k against 1,369k, which reads as stability. Underneath, single-family fell 7.7% while multifamily rose 21.8%. The 27bp mortgage backup from 6.14% to 6.41%, with the policy rate frozen at 3.50%, cut refinancing applications 31.8% between February and April and knocked the builder index to 34, its worst non-COVID March-to-April move since 2018, yet left purchase applications up 5.9%, squarely inside their normal seasonal range.

Indonesian e-money has passed half of electronic retail purchase value

The e-money share of card-plus-wallet purchase value ran 6.8% in Q1 2018, 45.8% in Q1 2025 and 55.8% in Q1 2026, reaching 57.7% in March alone, and is already 94.0% of transactions by count. The analyst rebuilt this on the purchase leg only after verifying that Bank Indonesia's headline e-money total double-counts by adding top-up to spending, and the component identity ties to 1.0000.

Indonesian cash is retreating to a store-of-value role

ATM cash withdrawal volume fell 9.6% quarter on quarter against the same quarter a year earlier, the steepest of the eight computable comparisons and more than twice the 4.4% Covid-era fall, a third consecutive annual decline. Meanwhile the average withdrawal ticket rose from IDR 725k in Q1 2018 to 1,058k: Indonesians withdraw less often and take more when they do. Debit purchase value fell 6.1% nominal, which is 9.6% real.

China's producer prices turned positive. Treat it as one month, not a turn

Chinese producer prices turned positive in March 2026 at +0.5% year on year, the first positive print since September 2022 after 41 months at or below zero. The challenge qualified this and we accept the qualification: March is seasonally the second-strongest month of the year for month-on-month PPI (mean +0.233 against +0.041 across all months), and two earlier Marches beat this one's +1.0: 2021 at +1.6 and 2022 at +1.1. The first read tested seasonality against only the 2023–25 Marches, all drawn from the deflation trough. A late Lunar New Year on 17 February is a named, untested confound: late-New-Year Marches average +0.575 month on month against −0.04 for early ones. What survives on any reading is that the construction-materials leg did not participate. Non-metal mineral products at −4.9%, a 43rd consecutive month below par and worse than March 2025's −3.4% in a year the headline improved 3.0 points. That removes the pricing-support leg from any SMGR or INTP thesis, and Chinese steel at −2.5% at the mill gate against inputs at +0.1% leaves no margin buffer, so expect continued export aggression into ASEAN.

The one large constructive Chinese signal is metals, and it is not a property signal

Across 88 price series the single unambiguous move is non-ferrous: mining +36.4% and smelting +22.4% year on year at March 2026, corroborated independently by CGPI mining products at +16.0%. That is constructive for Indonesian nickel, copper and bauxite exposure , INCO, MDKA, ANTM and the HPAL chain, and it is explicitly not evidence of Chinese property bottoming. Anyone selling it as that has the causation backwards.

The Chinese property overhang is frozen because supply and demand are shrinking together

The ratio of under-construction stock to that year's sold floor space has sat at 7.50, 7.53 and 7.49 years at the last three December year-ends, against 4.79 at December 2018. It is frozen not because supply is stable but because both sides are contracting: Q1 2026 residential floor space sold is −13.4% and residential sales value −18.7%, both from roughly flat a year earlier. Shrinking the numerator does not clear an overhang when the denominator shrinks faster, which is why this has not improved in three years and will not normalise toward 2018 levels before 2029 on the current path.

Chinese coal: the base has washed out, the demand has not arrived

Chinese coal producer prices moved from −23.0% in July 2025 to −2.2% in March 2026, which removes a large year-on-year drag from second-half comparatives for ADRO, ITMG and PTBA. That is arithmetic, not volume. Nothing in the construction data supports a Chinese demand-led coal price recovery: under-construction stock is contracting 11.7% and cement prices are still falling.

Nickel: the answer is upstream, and it changes where to sit in the chain

The Chinese non-ferrous price signal is strongly constructive , mining +36.4% and smelting +22.4%, while the volume chain that consumes Indonesian nickel is rolling over, with Chinese steel import volume at −13.6% in Q1. The China industry work partly resolves it. A measurement bug in our own tooling, found and fixed

The reviewer could not break a single computation in the bloc pack , the Fisher-z table reproduced to three decimals, and then found the measurement layer underneath it was wrong. Returns were indexed back N observations but labelled in days, so for monthly series the columns lied: the coal benchmark's “ret_5d +28.0%” was a 4.5-month move, and its “ret_63d +46.92%” was a sixty-two-month return sitting in the same column as the S&P's 63 trading days. Rates were percent-changed rather than differenced, which hid a 25bp ECB hike and a roughly 15bp fall in ten-year breakevens. Four different horizons shared a single column. Now fixed: windows are calendar-anchored and carry their base date, rates are differenced to basis points, and a sparsity guard refuses any window the series cannot cover. A monthly benchmark no longer reports a seven-day change at all. The ECB hike is now visible as +25bp from 3 May.

“The hedge never worked” has to be stated precisely: the JCI-to-S&P correlation explains 4.8% of daily variance unconditionally, and this data cannot test the tail. Normal-period correlation says little about crisis correlation, which is exactly when a hedge is called upon. The categorical version overstates what a full-sample daily correlation can support.

A China auto note published “Q1 2026 CNY 374.09bn, −13.61%” for domestic car retail value. How fourteen independent analysts made the same six mistakes

The mistakes weren't random. Six specific errors account for nearly all of them, and each one showed up across several analysts who never spoke to each other. That makes it a problem with how we prepared the data and how we read it, not with any one person. Which means it's fixable, and it has been fixed.

Truncated arrays read as full history

Several packs ship a recent array of the last 18 observations alongside a full series. Analysts read the short array as the whole record, producing claims like “the only contraction in the 18 months on file” for a series with 100 observations, and “frozen for all 18 months” for a price that had been raised inside the real history.

Percentiles quoted on trending nominal balances

A rupiah loan balance that has risen 132% over 100 months sits at the 99th percentile of its own history almost permanently. Four separate notes cited such a percentile as independent evidence of a cycle extreme. Only the growth rate carries information, and this trap was flagged in the first report and repeated here anyway.

Six-versus-six-month windows presented as year-on-year

Several packs compute change over trailing six months against the prior six. Notes then described these as annual changes. In the autos data, segment figures reverse sign when computed properly.

Religious-calendar shifts read as demand signals

Eid moved from 31 March 2025 to around 20 March 2026, and Ramadan from March to mid-February. Two of the most dramatic findings in this round. A retail sales contraction and a halving of payments growth , are both entirely this shift.

Seasonal steps read as regime changes

The coal export decline and the bank capital fall are both ordinary recurring seasonal moves, each matched or exceeded by the same month a year earlier.

Corroboration that is the same number twice

One note presented the rupiah leg of a credit series as independent confirmation of the total. The foreign-currency leg is 0.65% of the series, so the rupiah leg is the series.

The vintage conflict, resolved

One dispute was worth settling rather than reporting.

Several reviewers said the company financials in every industry file were dated 7 August, not 24 May, and that our whole "these numbers are ten weeks old" argument was therefore invented. One called it a fabricated date holding up the entire analysis.

They spotted something real and drew the wrong conclusion. Checking the source: all 636 Indonesian company rows carry updated_at = 2026-05-24. The financials genuinely are from 24 May. The 7 August date sits in a field called fundamentals_as_of, which is actually just the date our extract script ran. That's a naming bug in our own code, and it cost four analysts a correct call.

So the staleness point stands. Every multiple in this report predates the rate reversal. The field is now called extracted_at, and a real vintage field reads the date off the rows.

Indonesian growth, read for the first time

You asked what state Indonesia is in and where growth goes. Until this round the system couldn't answer, because Indonesian GDP had never been pulled out of the archive. A bug in our extract had dropped it, along with wages, employment, foreign investment, retail sales and 87 of 92 trade series. An earlier report told you "Indonesian GDP is not in the extracted set." It was there the whole time.

Growth is 5.6% and the number is solid. Real GDP grew 5.6085% in Q1. We checked it against CEIC's own separately published growth series and it matches to four decimal places. The eighteen industry components add up to the total exactly. Adjusted for the Ramadan timing shift it's 5.29% against a two-year average of 5.13%, so growth is genuinely picking up.

That timing adjustment matters more here than anywhere else, because 2026 is the first year in this entire dataset where Eid falls cleanly inside Q1. Two numbers flip without it. Hotels and restaurants print +13.14% raw and +9.24% adjusted. Inventories print +21.98% raw and −12.18% adjusted, which turns a positive contribution into −0.247pp. Read raw, we'd have told you growth was low-quality and inventory-driven. It's the opposite: Indonesia is running inventories down.

Government spending needs the same treatment: +21.81% raw against an austerity quarter last year, +7.05% adjusted.

Manufacturing is carrying it. On adjusted contributions to the 5.29%: manufacturing +1.096pp and speeding up, retail and wholesale +0.755pp, telecoms and IT +0.554pp, agriculture +0.471pp, construction +0.449pp, transport +0.402pp.

Mining is the only sector shrinking, at −0.88%, with two consecutive negative first quarters. That's the sector this book is most exposed to. It's stagnant rather than collapsing.

This contradicts something we've been saying. Manufacturing output is growing 5.41% and accelerating, while the PMI sits at 50.0. Those measure different things: PMI counts how many firms are expanding, output counts how much. A sector where big exporters grow and small firms stay flat looks exactly like this. Through Q1 Indonesian manufacturing output was not stalling, and we should stop citing PMI 50.0 as if it were.

Nobody had looked at the deflator. Nominal GDP grew 9.20% against real 5.61%, which puts the deflator at 3.40%. That's its sixth straight rise, from −0.84% in early 2024 through +2.00%, +2.30%, +2.43%, +2.93% to +3.40%. Prices across the whole economy were building before BI moved on 20 May. That makes the hike look less like defending the currency and more like responding to domestic inflation.

But 35% of growth sits in the most rate-sensitive thing there is. Fixed investment is 30.1% of GDP and contributed 1.850pp of the 5.290pp. We originally said it broke within two quarters of BI's last hiking cycle. It didn't: in 2022–23 construction slowed a quarter before the first hike, investment actually accelerated to +4.98% in the hike quarter, and both had recovered by Q3 2023 while BI was still tightening. So it's the most exposed component, but the last cycle doesn't say it breaks.

And the timing problem is absolute: there is no Q2 GDP in any dataset. Everything stops at 1 March. The rate move was 20 May.

The wage finding

Real wages in Indonesia's six biggest employment provinces have not moved in eight years. The index was set at 100 in February 2018. It reads 100.13 in February 2026, and sits 4.18% below its February 2020 peak.

Employment grew 1.30% over the year, but nearly all of it went to people with education. University +3.98%, vocational high school +3.11%. The 53.1% of the workforce with junior-high schooling or less grew 0.23%.

The headline unemployment rate of 4.68% and the strong income readings in the business survey are both nominal. In real terms nothing has happened for eight years.

That closes the loop on the mortgages. The people whose real pay hasn't risen since 2018 are the same people defaulting at twelve-year highs and being charged a record premium for consumer credit. This isn't a cyclical consumer problem. It's a distributional one that the credit cycle has now exposed.

The consumer started saving before the rate move

Reading the four months with no Ramadan or Eid distortion in either year, October to January, real retail sales grew 4.96% against 1.15% in the same window a year earlier. The consumer was accelerating.

Then in January, four months before BI moved, households cut spending to 71.96% of income from 74.94% and lifted savings to 17.86% from 14.42%. That's the biggest three-month jump in savings rate in the 96 months on file. Cheap-car sales fell to 39,292 units over January to April, the lowest in nine years.

Exports are all price, no volume

Over the twelve months to April, exports grew 5.90% in dollars on tonnage that fell 1.80%. Unit prices rose 7.85%. Every bit of export growth is price. If you hold export-levered Indonesian names, mark down the volume assumptions regardless of where commodity prices go.

One dispute worth settling: on palm oil, our first report said export earnings fell 26.3% and a reviewer countered that they rose 83.7%. Both used distorted windows. Combining March and April to cancel the Eid shift, animal and vegetable oils grew 8.4%, from USD 4,868mn to 5,275mn.

And the trade account doesn't explain the currency. The twelve-month goods surplus to February was USD 36.69bn, above the 35.09bn a year earlier. The rupiah's worst stretch, 16,758 on 28 February to 18,141 on 10 June, happened during the strongest export months of the year. Whatever is pressuring the currency is financial, not trade.

The funding shock

This is the freshest data here, running to 10 June, against industry numbers that mostly stop in April. We put it through a reviewer. Every figure checked out exactly. The explanation we built on top of it did not.

What checked out. Loan growth 9.4324%. Overnight rates 5.98307% on 10 June against 3.6554% on 22 January. Banks' spare cash at the central bank down to IDR 55,743bn on 9 June, with a low of 11,722 on 29 May. Working capital lending 7.9596% and investment lending 7.8628%, both the cheapest in the window. The gap between what banks charge households and corporates at 2.5688pp. Bank Syariah Indonesia's bad-debt provision jumping from IDR 11.09tn to 15.04tn in March and staying there in April. The reviewer reproduced all of it to the decimal.

Policy moved further than we first said. BI cut from 5.75% in April 2025 to 4.75% by September and held. Then the deposit rate went to 4.25% on 20 May and 4.50% on 9 June, the policy rate to 5.25%, and the whole corridor shifted. That's about 75bp of hikes and roughly 190bp of actual tightening in what banks pay overnight.

Our explanation was wrong. We said money was leaving the banking system for cash and dollars. Three of the four legs invert when you check them:

  • Money banks hold at the central bank grew 23.5%. Cash in circulation grew 15.8%. So money went into the banking system faster than into cash. The 73.8% cash share we flagged was 73.1% a year earlier and has been higher four times in the window.
  • Foreign currency deposits grew 8.94% while total money supply grew 9.22%, so the dollar share of deposits actually fell, from 13.71% to 13.67%. We quoted the two fastest-growing sub-categories, which are 46% of the total, and left out the biggest one, which is 54% and grew 0.55%. In dollar terms that shrank.
  • Cheap current-account deposits grew 21.31%, faster than the money supply. That's the cheapest funding a bank has, and there's more of it, which helps margins. What's actually scarce is expensive term deposits, growing 2.70%.

Two things we called stress were just seasonal. The March drop in bank capital is the annual dividend payment. It's the smallest such drop in six years: 2.86% this year against 6.65% in 2024 and 6.07% in 2025. And regional banks' loan-to-deposit ratio rising 7.85% over six months happens every September to March. Year on year that ratio is down 3.02%, and at 83.99% it's lower than commercial banks at 84.64% and state banks at 88.29%.

We also made a statistical error we criticise elsewhere. We said money supply growth was at the 96th percentile. That's the percentile of the raw amount, which climbs forever. The growth rate is at the 72.7th percentile.

What's left. The price of money moved hard, and that's verified. The margin squeeze is arithmetic on two confirmed numbers and holds. But deposits didn't run, and the deposit mix shifted toward cheaper funding, not away from it. One honest limit: lending rates stop at 1 April in every file we have. The margin claim compares a June funding cost to an April lending rate. We won't know if it's right until the next print.

What the two markets are pricing

The market fell 25.7% from its January peak. The index went from 180.655 on 1 January to 134.301 on 1 May. May was down 9.78%, March down 10.78%.

We said it was being dumped on heavy volume. It wasn't. May turnover was IDR 365,734bn, which is 47.6% below January's 698,211bn. Share volume was down 50.7%. Per trading day it's IDR 22,858bn against 34,911bn in January, 35% lower, and the second-thinnest month since August 2025. We had called it the 89th percentile, which is a percentile on a number that grows with inflation and market size. Monthly turnover averaged around 199,000bn in 2018 against roughly 446,000bn now, so almost any recent month looks high.

A market falling on thinning volume is buyers stepping back, not institutions selling out. That's a different problem and it fixes differently.

Foreigners are selling, and that part is real. Net foreign selling was IDR 53,972bn through May. That's already worse than the biggest full year in the data, which was 50,746bn in 2018.

The domestic bid didn't vanish either. We said local-to-local trading collapsed to 39.47% of turnover. The share is right, but it's a denominator problem. Local trading value rose 31.4% to IDR 144.4tn. Total turnover rose 45.6% and foreign-involved turnover rose 56.6%. Locals bought more than last year and got outpaced. May is also the weakest month of the year for this ratio, and 39.47% is only the third-lowest of nine Mays.

There's no stress signal in the bond market to match the equity fall. We said the curve had priced 75bp of stress. That 75bp was measured from 30 October 2025, which happens to be both the first day of our data window and the lowest yield in eight years of monthly data. Measured year on year, the 10-year is 6.858% against 6.865%. Flat.

We then said the flat long end meant term premium had vanished. That's also wrong. The 12-year rose 75bp and the 28-year rose 36bp while the policy rate went up, which is a normal bear flattener in a hiking cycle. And the flat bit past 22 years is the bond pricing model's own output, not real trades. Five tenors print 7.011746 to six decimal places.

The banks look cheap or the market is wrong. The 26 listed financials trade at 1.48 times book, banks at 1.33, on a 9.46% return on equity. Eleven of them, three-quarters of the sector by value, sit within 5% of their 52-week low. Back out what that implies for cost of equity and you get 17.7% for Bank Mandiri, 14.7% for BRI, 11.4% for BCA. Against a 7% government bond. BRI yields 13.70%, Mandiri 11.58%. Either those earnings are about to fall over, or those dividends get cut, or the market is wrong about Indonesian banks.

United States: the Fed already turned

We read five areas of US data. Three matter to this book. Every one has now been through a reviewer, and all three needed correcting.

The Fed started buying bonds again in December

The mechanics are exact. Quantitative tightening ended in December with the portfolio bottoming at $6,259bn. The Fed's Treasury bill holdings sat frozen at exactly $195bn from April 2024 through November 2025, then went $212bn, $254bn, $309bn, $366bn, $413bn. Bills did all of it: Treasuries rose $202bn from December to April, bills rose $201bn. Meanwhile mortgage bonds keep running off at about $14bn a month, so the Fed is adding cash to the banking system while deliberately shrinking its housing exposure.

Our explanation of why was wrong. We said reserves fell because the reverse repo facility emptied. Reserves fell because the Treasury rebuilt $401bn of cash between August and November. The repo facility draining $54bn was the only thing cushioning that.

The point isn't that the facility drained, it's that there was nothing left in it. In the same months of 2023, an $806bn drain from that facility let reserves rise $200bn even through a $288bn Treasury build and $223bn of tightening. By August 2025 only $54bn was left, so the next build hit reserves almost directly.

Two more corrections. The facility isn't at $0.6bn as we said. It holds $337bn, of which $336bn belongs to foreign central banks. We quoted one part as if it were the whole thing. And reserves fell 425.3bn, not the 406bn we published.

Producer prices are running well ahead of consumer prices

Consumer inflation is genuinely soft. The Cleveland Fed's median measure is 2.715%, and the one-month and six-month rates are below it, so it's still falling.

Producer prices are not. Excluding food, energy and trade margins, they're running 3.6% and accelerating from a 2.5% low. Firms say the same thing: the ISM manufacturing prices-paid index is 78.3, up 19.3 points since January and the highest since 2022.

On the energy shock, we've now been wrong in both directions. Report one said costs were being absorbed in margins. Report two said the opposite. Both were partly wrong. Retail fuel passed through completely: pump gasoline went $2.90 to $3.59 to $4.09 between February and April, and the CPI gasoline index rose 24.87% in the single month to 31 March, the biggest monthly move on record. Fuel retailers' margins actually widened. But it doesn't generalise. With fuel input costs up 6.79% then 7.14% month on month, finished goods prices excluding food and energy rose only 0.25% then 0.11%, and the margin measure had its two worst months of the cycle in exactly those two months. Someone is eating the cost. It's just not the petrol stations.

The job market is stalling, but not where we said

Payrolls grew 0.164% over the year. Of 902,000 jobs announced across twelve monthly reports, only 260,000 showed up in the level. The other 642,000 were revised away, with the gap negative in twelve of the last fourteen months. That's the finding that matters and it holds. Though the gap has been narrowing since December.

The concentration claim was misleading. Healthcare and education added 663,000 and leisure 176,000, but construction added 57,000, other services 35,000 and utilities 4,600, each growing faster in percentage terms than the total. And we said stripping healthcare leaves the US shedding 400,000 jobs a year. Of that 403,000, some 330,000 is federal government headcount, down 11% on the year. Private jobs outside healthcare are down 161,000, which is 0.15%. That's flat, not shrinking, and it's a very different statement.

What it means for Indonesia

The Fed has no reason to cut. Inflation expectations turned up in March and April on two unrelated measures, and long-run expectations never came back down through the whole 2025 disinflation.

The channel to this book is the long end, not the policy rate. The gap between 10-year yields and the Fed funds rate widened to +0.61pp from −0.05pp a year ago, and 30-year mortgages backed up to 6.41% while policy sat still. That's term premium rebuilding, and it competes directly with demand for emerging-market bonds. Foreign central banks now hold 17.2% of Treasury notes against 29.8% in 2019, so they aren't the marginal buyer any more. If your hedging assumes Treasuries rally when equities fall, that assumption needs testing.

China

China matters here because it's the end market for the commodities this book is exposed to.

Property has at least two more years to run

Development investment fell 11.2% in Q1 after 17.2% across 2025. New starts are 74% below the 2019 peak. But the number that tells you when this ends is the overhang: 5.42bn square metres under construction against 881m sold a year. That's 6.15 years of supply, and it has been stuck at roughly that level for three consecutive year-ends against 4.79 years in 2018.

It's stuck because both sides are shrinking together. Q1 residential floor space sold fell 13.4% and sales value 18.7%, both from roughly flat a year earlier. Building less doesn't clear an overhang when sales fall faster.

Producer prices turned positive, but not where it would help

China's PPI printed +0.5% in March, the first positive reading since September 2022 after 41 months at or below zero. Treat that as one month, not a turn. March is seasonally the second-strongest month of the year for producer prices, and two earlier Marches beat this one. Lunar New Year was also late this year, which pushes activity into March.

What holds regardless: cement, glass and concrete didn't participate. They're at −4.9%, a 43rd straight month of falling prices, and worse than a year ago in a year when the headline improved 3 points. That removes the pricing support from any Indonesian cement thesis.

Metals are the one clearly good signal, and it isn't about property

Across 88 Chinese price series the standout is non-ferrous metals: mining prices +36.4% and smelting +22.4%. That's backed by spending, not just price. Non-ferrous mining investment is +56.0% on sector revenue up 38.84%, which is a real boom rather than a price blip. Good for Indonesian nickel, copper and bauxite: INCO, MDKA, ANTM and the processing chain.

This is not evidence that Chinese property is bottoming. Anyone selling it that way has the causation backwards.

One thing we can't test: we said the downstream stainless chain is rolling over. The Chinese import series we used excludes ferronickel and nickel pig iron, which are the things Indonesia actually ships. So that leg is untested rather than confirmed. Copper is the cleaner exposure, holding on both price and volume, with concentrate volume up 6.5% into a 34.7% price rise.

Chinese coal demand has bottomed, but they're building capacity to need less

We said Chinese coal demand pointed down for Indonesian exporters, based on twelve-month import volume falling 9.2%. That's backward-looking. The same-quarter number is +1.2%, Q1 imports hit a record 116.3mt, and the trailing total turned up from a low of 484.25mt in November. Iron ore and crude also hit Q1 records. Volume risk is low.

The real risk is slower and nothing else found it. Chinese coal investment is up 25.5% on revenue up just 1.56%. Iron ore investment is up 20.1% on revenue down 7.08%. Spending that hard into flat revenue isn't a return-seeking cycle, it's state-directed self-sufficiency. Near-term volumes are fine. China is building the capacity to need less of what Indonesia sells.

Two more things worth knowing

Chinese companies are earning and refusing to reinvest. Industrial profits are up 15.5% while private investment is down 2.2%, the first negative outside 2020. Property investment is down 10.6% and nothing has replaced it: IT and software investment is down 6.1%, special-purpose equipment down 0.4%. The "rotation into advanced manufacturing" story is not in this data. That caps how much any stimulus routed through companies can do.

The trade surplus didn't really halve. March's surplus fell to USD 51.1bn from 101.9bn, which we reported as a collapse. Adjusted for Lunar New Year the Q1 surplus is 264.3bn against 271.1bn, down 2.5%. And of the Q1 import surge only 16.9% was raw materials: Switzerland, Australia and Hong Kong supplied a third of it between them, and Chinese central bank gold reserves only rose 7.2 tonnes, so the bullion was commercial.

We also said Chinese bond and equity markets had priced property in opposite directions. They haven't. The rising government share of the bond market has climbed every year since 2018, not since the property bust, and it's set by issuance quota rather than by anyone pricing risk. Our data has no yields or spreads in it at all.

What this means for the book

Our first report couldn't tell whether the three Indonesian positions shared a thesis or were three separate mistakes. The mortgage data answers it, and that answer held up under review.

Agung Podomoro and Paramita Bangun Sarana are one bet held twice. Both sit in Indonesian property and construction, and specifically in the mass-market segment where arrears are at twelve-year highs and mortgage lending has been shrinking for fourteen straight months. The premium end of that market is fine. Losses of 70% and 32% are what the credit data would predict. This isn't two bad calls, it's one exposure sized twice.

Two things make it worse. Foreigners have been steadily selling the Indonesian property complex, with LPKR the single largest distribution in the market. And the sector already trades at 0.46 times book on a 2.4% return on equity, so the market has marked it. There's nothing in the credit data that would re-rate it while mortgage lending is still shrinking and arrears are still climbing.

One correction. An earlier draft blamed a supply glut financed by a 46% surge in construction lending. That surge is two months of step-change in a series that was flat for the previous twelve, and we can't tell it apart from a reclassification. The demand-side and arrears evidence stands without it.

Coal, which took three attempts to get right

Report one implied a broad energy cost problem. Report two said coal was outside the shock and Indonesian exporters faced no cost pressure. Both were wrong. Here's what the data actually says:

  • Volumes are fine. Chinese Q1 coal imports hit a record 116.3mt and the same-quarter trend is +1.2%. Our −9.2% figure was a trailing twelve-month number that keeps falling mechanically as a weak base rolls off.
  • Prices are recovering, and unusually so. The Indonesian benchmark rose 18.3% from March to June, taking its year-on-year from −12.5% to +23.6%. That February-to-June stretch is normally negative, in six of the last eight years. A recovery running against its own seasonal pattern is harder to dismiss.
  • Costs are the problem, and that's the leg we denied. Pertamina Dex diesel rose 77% between February and April and 103% over the year, the biggest move since 2018. Diesel is the main input for mining and haulage.

So the coal position is supported on volume and price, and squeezed on margin. The US coal producer price both earlier versions leaned on is a domestic mine-mouth number with no thermal split. It tells you nothing about Indonesian costs either way.

The bigger question you haven't been asked

If banks' lending margins have halved in five months, and net foreign selling in five months already exceeds the worst full year on record, then the question isn't whether to hold two property names. It's whether any Indonesian equity position should be sized off company financials struck on 24 May, before the rate reversal and before overnight funding moved 233bp.

Method, assurance and limits

This work read the CEIC-style archive already sitting in your Supabase, which nothing had ever touched: 2,101 Indonesian macro series, 2,061 Indonesian industry series, 3,988 Chinese and 4,252 US. Filtered to things with 2026 data and enough history, that gave 84 category packs, joined to 636 Indonesian and 169 global companies to build 14 industry files. Twenty analysts read them independently, then reviewers were given the same data and told to break the results. Roughly 157 agents ran across six workflows.

How reliable is any given claim here? Each finding is tagged. Of 76 stored findings, all have now been checked either by an independent reviewer or directly against the source. Two-thirds of first drafts failed. So did eight of our corrections.

Where the data lives. About 37MB of extracts sit in a local folder, outside the repository and outside the database. Only the conclusions in this report reach Supabase and the terminal.

What we still can't tell you

There are no earnings estimates or analyst revisions anywhere in this data, so nothing here is forward-looking on company profits. The US national accounts, personal income and savings rate are missing, so the US consumption read is built from proxies. China has no capacity utilisation, orders or inventory data. There's no macro archive at all for Japan, Korea, Europe or India, so those desks run on market prices alone.

Problems we found in our own data and tools

Two files in the Indonesian set disagree about the current policy rate. Believe the daily one, which shows the May reversal.

Our extract wrote its own run date into a field called fundamentals_as_of, which made four analysts doubt a correct staleness claim. Renamed.

Several data packs ship a short 18-observation preview next to a 100-observation series, which produced at least two false "first time ever" claims. Every series now carries its true length and a warning if it's been cut.

Our bloc correlation tool measured returns by counting observations while labelling the columns in days, so a monthly coal series showed a 62-month return in a column marked 63 days. It also converted interest rates to percentages instead of differences, which hid a 25bp ECB hike. Fixed.

Our industry files shipped the top 26 companies by size, and sector shares were being calculated off that shortened list. That's what produced the PGUN 42.7% figure when the real number is 17.4%. Files now ship the full sector totals.

On the US side, two Congressional Budget Office series are presented as different measures but carry identical values. US metadata claims data starts in 2016 when it starts in 2018, which skews every percentile. And a Cleveland Fed inflation series is filed as if it were a Fed policy projection, which it isn't; there are no Fed projections in this data at all, so no US growth or rate forecast can come from it.

One recommendation we can't act on ourselves

The company financials are eleven weeks old and predate the rate reversal. Refreshing them is the single most valuable thing that could happen to this analysis. But nothing in the system populates those tables: no pipeline, no scheduled job, no API route. They're an external snapshot. This needs a data feed, which is a purchasing decision rather than something we can code.
