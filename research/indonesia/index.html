What survives

Report two told the CRO that Indonesian markets were showing distress. On the data, they are not. The claims that failed verification are precisely the ones that asserted it: that the IDX was falling on heavy volume and therefore being distributed, that the bond market had priced 75bp of stress, that manufacturing had rolled over, and that businesses had stopped hiring. Every one of those is wrong, and in most cases the data points the other way.

What survived is a squeeze in the price of funding — but not the mechanism we gave for it. Bank Indonesia reversed from cutting to hiking on 20 May, taking the policy rate to 5.25% and tightening the corridor a further 25bp after that. Overnight interbank went from 3.6554% on 22 January to 5.9831% on 10 June, +233bp, and now trades 148bp above the floor at the 100th percentile of its history when in January it traded below the floor. Excess reserves sit at the 20th percentile. Working capital lending printed 7.9596% on 1 April, the lowest in the window, priced for a cutting cycle that ended seven weeks later. Every one of those figures reproduced exactly on challenge.

The deposit-flight story we published to explain it did not. Three of its four legs invert when checked. Bank reserves at Bank Indonesia grew 23.5% year on year against currency in circulation at 15.8%, so base money went into the banking system faster than into cash. The foreign-currency share of M2 fell to 13.67% from 13.71% a year earlier, below its 2024 peak, so there is no dollarisation in this data. And rupiah transferable deposits — the cheapest bank funding there is — grew 21.31%, faster than M1 itself, which is margin-accretive and cuts against our own compression thesis. Only the expensive time deposits are scarce, at 2.70%.

The currency pressure is real, and it is not a dollar story. USD/IDR reached 18,171 on 9 June, the weakest print in the sample, +9.44% year on year, and the five worst twenty-day depreciation windows in the entire 140-session record all fall between 4 and 10 June. But the rupiah fell against the yuan (+16.0%), the euro (+12.3%), the Singapore dollar (+10.3%) and sterling (+9.0%) as well, while the dollar itself was weak against the yuan and the euro. Both effective-rate constructions sit at their sample lows. Depreciation was accelerating when the daily data went dark.

So: the price of funding moved sharply and the currency is under broad pressure, but the deposit base did not run. That is a materially less alarming configuration than the one we published, and it changes what to watch.

What survives verification

  1. Monetary policy reversed, further than first reported, and one of our data files still does not know it. The deposit facility stepped 3.75% to 4.25% on 20 May and to 4.50% on 9 June, the policy rate went to 5.25%, and the corridor tightened a further 25bp after that. The banking archive file still carries 5.50/3.75 with a last observation of 30 April and shows no reversal at all. Believe the daily series; it has the later observation.
  2. Overnight funding moved 233bp and the banks' marginal spread halved. IndONIA went from 3.6554% on 22 January to 5.9831% on 10 June, from trading below the deposit floor to 148bp above it at the 100th percentile of the overlap. Working capital lending printed 7.9596% on 1 April, the window low, priced for a cutting cycle that ended seven weeks later, so the marginal spread is roughly 198bp against 421bp on 1 January. Excess reserves sit at the 20th percentile.
  3. The mechanism is not deposit flight — that was our error, and it inverts. Report two argued base money was leaking out of the banking system into cash and dollars. Checked against the same packs: bank deposits at Bank Indonesia grew 23.5% year on year against currency in circulation at 15.8%, so reserves grew faster than cash, and the 73.8% cash share was 73.1% a year earlier and has been higher four times in the window. The foreign-currency share of M2 fell to 13.67% from 13.71%; the two fast sub-lines we quoted are 46% of the FX stock, and the largest component, FX transferable deposits at 54%, grew 0.55% — in dollar terms a contraction. And rupiah transferable deposits grew 21.31%, faster than M1, so the cheapest funding is the abundant kind. What is genuinely scarce is time-deposit funding at 2.70%. The currency pressure is real and broad — USD/IDR at 18,171 on 9 June is the sample weak point and the rupiah fell against the yuan, euro, Singapore dollar and sterling too — but it is not showing up as a run on rupiah deposits.
  4. The consumer credit tail is deteriorating inside an aggregate that is healthy. Report two conflated two series here, and checking the full database history makes the correction firmer. The system NPL ratio is 2.119% at 1 March and down from 2.138% a year earlier. Against the whole 279-month record back to January 2003 — not the eight-year window our extract ships — that sits against an all-time maximum of 8.420%. Indonesian system asset quality is at a twenty-three-year best. The genuine crack is narrower and sharper: consumption NPLs against individual loans are 1.498%, the 99th percentile of their own window against a 1.065% median, and the consumption NPL stock at IDR 53,855bn is within 0.2% of the all-time high it set the month before, on loan growth of only 3.0%.
  5. The Indonesian mortgage market has split by segment, and this is the strongest verified finding in the programme — stronger, in fact, than we first reported. It has survived two independent adversarial passes, and re-checking it against the full database history rather than the 100-month window our extract ships makes it firmer, not weaker. Across 148 monthly observations back to January 2014, three mass-market segments are at all-time highs: apartment mortgage arrears at 4.161% against a 2014–2017 maximum of 2.615%, apartment 22–70m² at 4.786% against 2.946%, and landed sub-21m² at 6.416% against 3.623%. Each set its record in the latest month, and the six highest apartment observations are the last six months in order. The premium end is not merely calmer, it is below its own pre-2018 peak: landed above 70m² at 3.058% against a 3.176% pre-2018 maximum and a 4.204% high set in July 2020. Apartment mortgage growth has fallen monotonically for fourteen straight months, from 8.19% in February 2025 to −0.03% in April 2026.
    One caveat found in the same check and worth keeping: the smallest apartment segment, sub-21m², is at 6.391% and is not a record — it reached 10.203% in July 2018. The mass-market break is real and broad, but that particular cohort has been worse before.
  6. The IDX drawdown is real and is not distribution, but our explanation of it was noise. The index is 25.7% below its January peak, on thinning volume: May turnover is 47.6% below January and 35% lower per trading day. That is a buyers' strike and a domestic de-rating, not institutional distribution. We then attributed it to the JCI decoupling from every other bloc — correlation work we computed correctly and never tested for significance. Nine of the ten cross-bloc shifts are indistinguishable from sampling noise (p=0.11 to 0.79); only Europe-China clears an unadjusted 5% test, and none survives correction for ten simultaneous tests. The practical conclusion survives for a different and simpler reason: a global index hedge would not have protected the book because the JCI-to-S&P correlation is only 0.22 across 1,778 common days, an R-squared of 4.8%. The hedge never worked; nothing broke. Stop expecting it to come back.
  7. One Indonesian name has a valuation set by almost no trading — not four, and our own extract inflated one of the figures. DCI Indonesia is the real case: 44.9% of listed technology at 476x earnings on IDR 195mn of daily turnover. PANI is 41.4% of property capitalisation on a 15.9% free float at 80.6x, but it trades IDR 41.4bn a day, the most of any IDX property stock, so it does not belong in an illiquidity list at all. PGUN is 17.4% of the 35-name plantation sub-sector, not the 42.7% this report published — that figure was an artifact of our own top-26-by-market-capitalisation truncation, which left only three plantation names in the denominator. And Bayan is 19.4% of energy, or 39.9% of coal on the most generous cut, so it clears 40% of nothing.
  8. The United States restarted balance-sheet expansion in December 2025 — verified, but our explanation of why was wrong. The mechanics reproduce exactly: quantitative tightening ended in December with the portfolio bottoming at $6,259bn, the bill book went from a frozen 195 to 212, 254, 309, 366 and 413bn, and bills did 100% of the work — Treasuries rose $202bn from December to April against bills at $201bn, with the coupon book flat once inflation compensation is netted — alongside roughly $14bn a month of mortgage-backed runoff. The causal story does not survive. Reserves did not fall because the reverse repo facility emptied. They fell because the Treasury rebuilt $401bn of cash between August and November, and the money-fund leg draining $54bn was the only thing cushioning it. The load-bearing fact is the buffer's exhaustion rather than its drain: in the same months of 2023, an $806bn drain let reserves rise $200bn straight through a $288bn Treasury build and $223bn of tightening; by August 2025 only $54bn was left. Nor is the facility at $0.6bn — it holds $337bn, of which $336bn is the foreign official pool; we quoted one leg as if it were the whole. And “one for one” is already wrong going forward: since December the Treasury account rose $16bn while reserves rose $102bn, because $147bn of Fed purchases outran it.

What this means for the board

The Indonesian case is no longer that the economy is contracting. Real GDP grew 5.6085% in Q1, activity data improved year on year, the manufacturing index is above last year and turned up in the latest print, and system asset quality is at a twenty-three-year best measured across the full 279-month record. The case is that the price of funding changed sharply in May and June, the currency is under broad pressure against every major cross rather than just the dollar, and a specific consumer credit cohort is failing while real wages for the bottom half of the workforce have not moved in eight years. None of the real-economy data has yet absorbed any of the funding move.

A measurement bug in our own tooling, found and fixed

The reviewer could not break a single computation in the bloc pack — the Fisher-z table reproduced to three decimals — and then found the measurement layer underneath it was wrong. Returns were indexed back N observations but labelled in days, so for monthly series the columns lied: the coal benchmark's “ret_5d +28.0%” was a 4.5-month move, and its “ret_63d +46.92%” was a sixty-two-month return sitting in the same column as the S&P's 63 trading days. Rates were percent-changed rather than differenced, which hid a 25bp ECB hike and a roughly 15bp fall in ten-year breakevens. Four different horizons shared a single column. Now fixed: windows are calendar-anchored and carry their base date, rates are differenced to basis points, and a sparsity guard refuses any window the series cannot cover — a monthly benchmark no longer reports a seven-day change at all. How fourteen independent analysts made the same six mistakes

The failures were not random. Six specific errors account for almost all of them, and each recurred across several independent analysts who had no contact with one another. That makes this a property of the data packs and the method rather than of any individual reader, which in turn makes it fixable.

Truncated arrays read as full history

Several packs ship a recent array of the last 18 observations alongside a full series. Analysts read the short array as the whole record, producing claims like “the only contraction in the 18 months on file” for a series with 100 observations, and “frozen for all 18 months” for a price that had been raised inside the real history.

Percentiles quoted on trending nominal balances

A rupiah loan balance that has risen 132% over 100 months sits at the 99th percentile of its own history almost permanently. Four separate notes cited such a percentile as independent evidence of a cycle extreme. Only the growth rate carries information, and this trap was flagged in the first report and repeated here anyway.

Six-versus-six-month windows presented as year-on-year

Several packs compute change over trailing six months against the prior six. Notes then described these as annual changes. In the autos data, segment figures reverse sign when computed properly.

Religious-calendar shifts read as demand signals

Eid moved from 31 March 2025 to around 20 March 2026, and Ramadan from March to mid-February. Two of the most dramatic findings in this round — a retail sales contraction and a halving of payments growth — are both entirely this shift.

Seasonal steps read as regime changes

The coal export decline and the bank capital fall are both ordinary recurring seasonal moves, each matched or exceeded by the same month a year earlier.

Corroboration that is the same number twice

One note presented the rupiah leg of a credit series as independent confirmation of the total. The foreign-currency leg is 0.65% of the series, so the rupiah leg is the series.

The vintage conflict, resolved

One conflict was serious enough to resolve directly rather than report. Several challengers asserted that the equity fundamentals in every industry pack carry fundamentals_as_of: 2026-08-07, that no 24 May vintage exists anywhere in the files, and that therefore the entire “multiples are ten weeks stale and have not seen the shock” argument is void. One challenger called it a fabricated, load-bearing date.

They were reading a real defect and drawing the wrong conclusion from it. Checked directly against the source: all 636 Indonesian rows carry updated_at = 2026-05-24. The fundamentals genuinely are struck on 24 May. The fundamentals_as_of: 2026-08-07 field is the date the extract script ran, written into a field whose name says otherwise. That is a naming bug in our own tooling, and it misled four independent analysts into doubting a correct claim.

So the staleness argument stands: every multiple in this report predates the policy reversal and the interbank move. The field has been renamed to extracted_at and a genuine fundamentals_as_of now carries the row-level vintage.

Indonesian growth, read for the first time

The CRO asked what the state of Indonesia is and where growth is going. Until this round the system could not answer, because Indonesian GDP had never been extracted — a truncation bug in our own tooling had dropped it along with wages, employment, FDI, retail and 87 of 92 trade series. Five of the seven notes written on that recovered data were then put to adversarial review and came back flawed. What follows is the corrected version, and the corrections are set out rather than absorbed.

Indonesia is growing fast, and this part is independently confirmed. Real GDP grew 5.6085% year on year in Q1 2026 on the 2010 constant-price series, matching CEIC's separately published real-growth series to four decimal places, with the eighteen industry components reconciling to the total exactly. It is the fastest print since Q3 2022's 5.7315%. Growth is broad, and mining and quarrying is the only contracting sector at −0.88% over the trailing year.

Three corrections to what this report published two revisions ago

The “Lebaran-neutral 5.29%” was not a calendar control. A four-quarter rolling ratio is arithmetically identical to the four-quarter mean of the year-on-year prints, and those prints are 5.1224, 5.0393, 5.3885 and 5.6085, whose mean is 5.2897. The figure is a moving average of an accelerating series, and the gap between 5.61% and 5.29% is simply the distance from the latest quarter to its own trailing-year mean. Worse, the stated justification is false in a way that runs against us: any four-quarter window contains one, two or zero Eids depending on where the boundary falls, and this is a boundary pair. The current window holds one Eid; the base window holds two, on 10 April 2024 and 31 March 2025. The “control” is therefore more unbalanced than the raw comparison it replaced, and the extra Eid in the denominator biases it downward.

And the raw comparison needed almost no adjustment anyway. This report asserted that 2026 is the first year in the window with Eid cleanly inside Q1. Eid 2025 fell on 31 March 2025 — inside Q1 2025. Ramadan sat wholly inside the first quarter in both years. Both quarters contain the full fasting month and Eid day, and the residual difference is roughly one post-Eid holiday week. So the three findings this report claimed the control had rescued were attributed to a calendar shift that largely did not occur between these two quarters.

The inventory conclusion was backwards. This report said Indonesia is drawing inventories down rather than building them. Change in stock over the trailing year to March 2026 is +232,954.7 IDR bn against +265,268.9 a year earlier. Both are large positive accumulations. Inventory building slowed by 12.2%; it never reversed. A drawdown requires the flow to go negative, which it does in individual quarters but in neither annual window. Having warned against a false “inventory-led growth” reading, this report published its mirror image.

What the corrected data supports

The deflator is still the finding nobody had looked at, and it is longer-running than we said. Nominal GDP grew 9.1991% against real 5.6085%, giving a deflator of 3.4000% — the ninth consecutive quarterly acceleration off a −1.3019% trough in December 2023, not the sixth off −0.84% as published. Economy-wide price pressure was building steadily before Bank Indonesia reversed on 20 May, which makes the hike look less like a currency defence and more like a response to domestic prices.

The claim that a funding shock reliably breaks fixed investment is not supported by these packs. Gross fixed capital formation is 30.10% of Q1 GDP, growing 5.96%. This report framed it as the component that broke within two quarters of the last hiking cycle. The 2022–23 analogue says otherwise: construction decelerated a quarter before BI's first hike, GFCF accelerated to +4.98% in the hike quarter itself, and both had recovered to +6.39% and +5.08% by Q3 2023 while BI was still tightening. The exposure is real and worth watching; the mechanism we asserted for it is not in the data.

Manufacturing still contradicts the PMI reading. Manufacturing gross value added is running +5.41% over the trailing year and accelerating, contributing 1.096pp. The PMI is a diffusion index measuring the breadth of expansion across firms; GVA measures volume, and a concentrating sector where large exporters grow while small firms stay flat produces exactly this signature. Through Q1 2026 Indonesian manufacturing output was not stalling.

There is no Q2 2026 GDP in any pack. Every series stops at 1 March; the shock began on 20 May. The entire shock period is outside the national accounts, and the leading indicators split — the normalised composite indicators fall while the trend-restored versions of the same OECD series rise every month through February.

The household leg, corrected

Real wages have gone nowhere in eight years, but the precision we published was false. The mass-employment province index is +0.1% to +1.0% cumulative since February 2018 depending on weighting, and −0.3% on a seasonally clean February-plus-August basis. The direction holds; the “exactly flat at 100.13” framing implied a precision the weighting choice does not support.

The distributional split is real and is better stated this way: 90% of the +1.30% year-on-year employment gain went to the 47% of workers with at least general senior-high schooling, while the 53% with junior high or less took 10% of it.

And participation is falling, not rising. The last four rounds print 70.63, 70.60, 70.59, 70.56. This report had it the wrong way round. Two other household claims also fail: the income-minus-durables gap is no wider than it was in 2023 (16.20 against 15.08), and the affluent-versus-mass debt-service divergence exists only against a 2024 base and reverses against 2023.

The consumer, corrected

The clean-window reading survives: across October 2025 to January 2026, the only four months with no Ramadan or Lebaran in either year, real retail sales grew a mean 4.96% against 1.15% a year earlier. But the level is the problem. The index in that window is only 1.25% above the same months in 2019–20, which on population is a per-capita decline, and the series prints −1.92% in April 2026, its last observation.

The precautionary-saving finding needs its base stated. The household savings share did rise to a February-April mean of 17.86% from 14.42%, on the largest three-month step in the data. But 14.42% was the lowest base in the array, and 17.86% remains below the 19.73% of 2019 and the 21.38% of 2018. This is normalisation from a post-COVID trough, not a new precautionary regime.

The vehicle composition claim collapses entirely. Mass-market LCGC units did fall to 39,292 over January to April, a nine-year low, down 19.0% year on year. But no claim about the market's composition can be made from this pack: the seven itemised segments cover 41.1% of retail units, and the unitemised 58.9% — dominated by the 4x2 non-LCGC passenger cars that are Indonesia's actual mass-market household vehicle — grew 10.5% and supplied 86% of the entire unit increase. This report's earlier reading of a fleet-led recovery was made without observing the segment that produced it.

Trade, corrected

The palm dispute settles at about +5%, not +8.4%. The +8.4% figure is the animal and vegetable oils chapter; palm itself underperforms that chapter by 2.4 to 6.4 points in every window where both are observed, implying roughly +5.1% for palm. Both the −26.3% report two published and the +83.7% its challenger countered with remain refuted.

“All export growth is price” is a mix effect, not a price effect. The arithmetic reproduces — value +5.48% on volume −2.84% year to date — but the inference does not. Coal value fell USD 716mn over January to March, which at prevailing coal prices accounts for 88% to 132% of the entire tonnage decline. Coal is roughly 37% of export tonnage. What looks like an economy-wide price-not-volume story is substantially one commodity shrinking.

The rupiah case is directionally right and weaker than we made it. The trade account is not the driver, but the twelve-month surplus is above its year-ago level only because ten of those months predate the currency move: the January-plus-February 2026 run-rate is USD 1.11bn a month against 3.29bn a year earlier. And no import data exists after February 2026, so the balance across the currency's worst months is unobserved, not improving. The conclusion stands on the cross-rate evidence — the rupiah fell against the yuan, euro, Singapore dollar and sterling while the dollar itself was weak — not on the trade balance.

The funding shock

This is the freshest data in the report and the most important. It runs to 10 June against an industry dataset that mostly stops in April. It has now been adversarially challenged, and the split is clean: every descriptive figure reproduced exactly; the causal mechanism did not.

What reproduced. Total loan growth 9.4324%. IndONIA 5.98307% on 10 June against a 3.6554% low on 22 January. The corridor spread at +1.48307pp over 122 sessions. Excess reserves of IDR 55,743bn with a window minimum of 11,722 on 29 May. Working capital lending at 7.9596% and investment lending at 7.8628%, both window minima. The consumption-minus-working-capital rate gap at 2.5688pp. The Bank Syariah Indonesia impairment step from IDR 11,090,997mn to 15,039,360mn in March, held at 15,043,061mn in April. The reviewer reproduced all of it to the decimal.

Policy reversed, and further than first reported. Bank Indonesia cut from 5.75% in April 2025 to 4.75% by September and held through April 2026. The policy rate then went to 5.25% on 20 May, the whole corridor shifted 50bp that day, and a further 25bp landed on 9 June — roughly 75bp of hikes plus about 190bp of effective overnight tightening. IndONIA confirms it bit: 3.9–4.2% through April, 5.15% on 20 May, 5.98% on 10 June.

What did not survive is the explanation. Report two attributed the squeeze to deposit flight into cash and foreign currency. Three of the four legs invert:

  • Bank deposits at Bank Indonesia went from IDR 338,288bn to 417,628bn, +23.5% year on year, against currency in circulation at +15.8%. Base money went into the banking system faster than into cash. The 73.76% cash share is the 77th percentile of the window, not an extreme: it was 73.1% in the same month a year earlier and has printed 74.0%, 74.8%, 74.1% and 77.9% before.
  • Total foreign-currency deposits grew 8.94% against M2 at 9.22%, so the FX share of M2 fell, to 13.67% from 13.71%, below its 14.18% peak in May 2024. We quoted the two fastest sub-lines, 46% of the stock, and omitted the largest: FX transferable deposits, 54% of it, grew 0.55%, which is a contraction in dollar terms given USD/IDR rose 3.20% over the same year.
  • Rupiah transferable deposits grew 21.31%, faster than M1 at 19.26%, and savings deposits 7.08%. The cheapest funding is the fastest-growing. That mix shift is margin-accretive, which cuts directly against the compression thesis it was cited to support. What is scarce is expensive time-deposit funding at 2.70%.

Two prudential “stress” readings were seasonal. The March capital-adequacy drop of 2.86% is the annual dividend appropriation, and it is the mildest in six years: the same month took 2.96% in 2020, 4.37% in 2022, 4.82% in 2023, 6.65% in 2024 and 6.07% in 2025. The regional-bank loan-to-deposit rise of 7.85% over six months is the recurring September-to-March move (+9.31% in 2019-20, +6.75% in 2024-25), and year on year that ratio is down 3.02%, from 86.61% to 83.99% — below commercial banks at 84.64% and well below state-owned banks at 88.29%. Ranking cohorts by percentile against their own different distributions is not a like-for-like stress comparison.

And one more percentile error of our own. Report two put reserve-money growth “at the 96th percentile”. That is the percentile of the nominal level — the exact move the same note correctly rejected one paragraph earlier for loan balances. The growth rate of 14.758% sits at the 72.7th percentile of its own history.

What this leaves. The price of funding moved hard and is verified. The banks' marginal spread — overnight funding at 5.9831% against working capital lending at 7.9596%, so roughly 198bp against 421bp on 1 January — is arithmetic on two confirmed numbers and stands. But the funding base did not run, and the deposit mix moved toward cheaper money rather than away from it. One caution the reviewer raised and this report accepts: lending and deposit rate series stop at 1 April in every file in the archive, so nothing after the 20 May reversal is directly observable. The margin claim is an inference from the gap between a June funding rate and an April lending rate, and it cannot be confirmed either way until the next print.

The single most load-bearing fact in this thesis has now been checked directly. The daily Bank Indonesia deposit facility series prints 3.75% from 27 November 2025, steps to 4.25% on 20 May 2026 and to 4.50% on 9 June — both dates and both levels reproduce precisely. The monthly series corroborates, 3.75% from 1 September to 4.25% at 1 May. Everything downstream of the reversal rests on this, and it stands on a direct read of the primary series rather than on any analyst's summary.

What the two markets are pricing

The index. IDX share price (2015=100) printed 134.301 at 1 May against 180.655 at 1 January, a 25.7% drawdown in four months. Month-on-month was −9.78% in May and −10.78% in March; year-on-year flipped from +24.50% in January to −6.06% in May.

It is falling on thinning volume, which is the opposite of what report two said. May turnover value was IDR 365,734bn against January's 698,211bn, down 47.6%, on 612,768mn shares against 1,243,448mn, down 50.7%. Per trading day that is IDR 22,858bn against 34,911bn, 35% lower, and the second-lowest month since August 2025. Report two read the 89th-percentile ranking as heavy volume; that percentile is computed against a nominal series whose monthly average has risen from roughly 199,000bn in 2018 to a ~446,000bn trailing-year mean, so almost any recent month ranks high. The turnover-to-index ratio fails for the same reason and worse: it is not bounded, it is mechanically inflated when the denominator falls 25.7%, and its series maximum is January 2026 — the index high. A measure that peaks at the top is not a distress signal.

Thinning volume into a 25.7% fall is a buyers' strike, not distribution. It is a materially less alarming reading, and it fits the foreign-flow data better.

Foreign selling is large cumulatively and easing at the margin. Net foreign purchase was −IDR 53,972bn year to date through May, which does exceed the largest full-year outflow in the sample (−IDR 50,746bn in 2018). But March was the window minimum at −IDR 23,340bn and May was only −IDR 4,097bn — the smallest monthly outflow in three months, in the month prices fell hardest. Foreign holdings of Indonesian government bonds are down just 1.3% over seven months. And the domestic bid did not vanish, which is another claim this report got wrong. Domestic-to-domestic crossing is 39.47% of turnover, genuinely the 12.9th percentile against a 49.86% median — but that is a denominator effect. Domestic crossing value rose 31.4% year on year to IDR 144.4tn; total turnover rose 45.6% and foreign-involved turnover 56.6%. The domestic bid grew and was outgrown. May is also seasonally the weakest month for this ratio, and 39.47% ranks only third-lowest of nine Mays.

There is no corroborating stress in the bond market, and the flat curve is not what we said it was either. Report two claimed the curve had priced 75bp of stress, measured from 30 October 2025 — both the first observation of a truncated window and the minimum of the monthly series. Year on year the ten-year is 6.858% against 6.865%: minus 0.6bp, flat. We then read the flat 12-to-28-year shape as a vanished term premium. On verification that fails too. It is a bear flattener into a hiking cycle: the twelve-year rose 75bp and the twenty-eight-year 36bp over the window while the policy rate went 4.75% to 5.25%. The flat 22-to-28-year tail is a fitted-curve asymptote — five tenors printing 7.011746 to six decimal places — not five market observations. And the 0.1bp is one day inside a three-month range of 0 to 7bp that reached −14.5bp in March.

Issuance has moved onshore. Local corporate bond issues outstanding reached 1,456 at 1 May, +30.9% year on year, while international issues fell to 82, −11.8% and down from a window maximum of 115. The local-to-international ratio of 17.76 is the highest in the window.

On bank valuation, report two overstated the spread. Solving the price-to-book identity at 5% terminal growth does give 17.7% for BMRI, 14.9% BNGA, 14.7% BBRI, 13.0% BBNI and 11.4% BBCA. But across all twenty listed banks only six fall in the 13–18% band, together 43.9% of set market capitalisation, and the largest name — BBCA at 29.6% of the set — is 11.4%. The tail runs far lower: BNLI 6.4%, PNBN 4.9%, ARTO 4.1%, BTPN −4.9%. The capitalisation-weighted big-four figure is 13.8%. Eleven of the 26 listed financials, 73.5% of set capitalisation, still sit within 5% of their 52-week low, and the capitalisation-weighted dividend yield is 8.08%, with BBRI at 13.70% and BMRI at 11.58%.

So the honest version of report two's closing line: the equity market has de-rated the banks hard on thinning volume while the bond market has not moved at all. That is still a disagreement worth resolving, but it is a disagreement between a de-rating and indifference, not between a recession and 75bp of stress.

The most current activity data available

This section reverses report two's reading. The forward indicators do not show a deteriorating economy; on a proper year-on-year basis they show an improving one.

Manufacturing. The purchasing managers' index is 50.0 seasonally adjusted in May, which report two described as having given back an entire expansion. The February-to-May fade is a recurring seasonal pattern that was larger in both prior years: 2025 fell 6.7 points over the same stretch against 2026's 3.8. January-to-May means run 52.96 in 2024, 50.40 in 2025 and 51.12 in 2026. The index is above last year and turned up in the latest print.

Business and consumer surveys. Report two wrote that businesses expect to sell more while hiring almost nobody. The level is right and the direction is the opposite. Year on year, almost everything improved: business activity +3.1, usage of labour +0.9 from a negative base, present employment +7.1, expected employment +4.3, headline consumer confidence +1.3, current income +2.7, and the OECD leading indicator +0.2. Only five of fifteen load-bearing series fell, and the clean negatives are narrow and specific: expected business conditions −4.4 at the 19th percentile, forward selling-price intentions −1.9, and a durables-versus-income gap at −15.5.

Hiring intentions are stuck near zero, but as a level that has stopped getting worse rather than a fresh deterioration. The genuine signal in this dataset is a discretionary-versus-staples split inside the consumer, which is consistent with the consumer credit data, and not a broad macro slowdown.

How report two got it wrong is worth recording, because it is the same error the report criticised elsewhere. The original note compared 2026 against a 2018–2024 matched-quarter baseline, which is a comparison of levels, and then described the result as a second-derivative story. The level story is real. The second-derivative story is the reverse of what the data shows.

The activity composite built from seven families of Indonesian indicators reads flat at a median z of 0.31, with tax revenue, bank lending, consumer confidence and money supply positive and payments volume, consumer expectations and exports negative. Given how much of this programme's survey work turned out to be calendar and base effects, that composite should be treated as a weak prior.

All of this predates the funding shock. None of these surveys has absorbed a 233bp move in overnight rates.

Indonesia against the world

The first report had no notion of value at all: every stance came from price and macro drivers. This is the missing layer, computed across 612 Indonesian and 168 global names, with value, quality and growth z-scores calculated within sector so that a bank is not ranked against a software company.

Indonesia trades at roughly a third of global multiples. Indonesian property is at 0.46x book against global real estate at 2.72x, which the verified mortgage-arrears data says is deserved for the mass-market names. Indonesian financials are at 0.76x book against global financials at 2.33x, which is partly deserved, since the deterioration is concentrated in consumer books rather than in the corporate lenders.

Three caveats before anyone acts on a multiple. The two fundamentals tables carry different vintages, which this report previously stated as one: all 636 Indonesian rows are struck 24 May, all 169 global rows 31 May, so every IDX-versus-global multiple below compares marks seven days apart. Both predate the policy reversal and the interbank move entirely. The Indonesian Energy sector's median price-to-book of 15,556 in the source data is corrupted and must not be quoted. And several global names including Altria and McDonald's carry negative book value from buybacks, which makes price-to-book meaningless for them rather than attractive.

The concentration problem deserves more weight than the discount. Four names each represent 40% or more of their sector's market capitalisation on turnover that could not clear a single day's position: DCI Indonesia at 44.9% of technology on IDR 195mn of daily trade, PGUN at 42.7% of plantation on IDR 110mn, PANI at 42.3% of property on a 15.9% free float, and Bayan at IDR 333tn on IDR 0.48bn. Any statement of the form “Indonesian sector X trades at Y” is a statement about these four names before it is a statement about the sector.

Where quality is genuinely available at a low price the list is short. United States: the Fed already turned

The US archive was re-read this round on data that was never available before: our own extractor had dropped roughly half of it, and two report-two notes were written around series they wrongly believed did not exist. All five notes below were produced on the complete packs.

The Fed restarted balance-sheet expansion in December 2025, and the causal chain is fully visible. Between July and November 2025 the Treasury General Account rebuilt from $346bn to $922bn, a $576bn drain. The reverse repo facility, which had absorbed such drains for three years, could only supply $196bn: its money-fund leg fell from $202bn in July 2025 to $6bn by November and $0.6bn by April 2026, down 99.6% year on year. Reserves absorbed the rest, falling $406bn across September, October and November to $2,888bn. The Standing Repo Facility then printed non-zero for the first time since July 2020 and discount window credit reached the 80th percentile at double its year-ago level. Securities held outright stopped shrinking in December after twenty straight declines, then grew $25bn, $40bn, $43bn and $39bn — almost all bills, the book going from a frozen $195bn to $212bn, $254bn, $309bn, $366bn, $413bn. That is reserve management, not stimulus, and it runs alongside continued mortgage-backed runoff of roughly $14bn a month. With the reverse repo facility at $0.6bn there is no buffer left: the next Treasury cash build hits bank reserves one for one.

The household question report two could not answer. It inferred that nominal spending running about 1.8pp above nominal income implied a falling saving rate, and said it could not verify this because the series was absent. The series was never absent. Checked directly: the saving rate is 4.0% in February 2026 against 5.2% a year earlier, the 11.7th percentile of its window — so the mechanism is right. The number is not: the true twelve-month mean gap is +0.90pp, and the 1.8pp is reproducible as chain-store sales (+6.14%) minus nominal disposable income (+4.22%), an artifact of the proxy report two was forced to use. The sharper facts are that real disposable income per capita has stalled at $52,837, +0.71% year on year but +0.03% annualised over six months, and that real consumption has split hard: services +3.04% against goods +1.18%, with goods volumes contracting sequentially at −3.51% annualised.

The energy shock went to the consumer, not into margin. Report two concluded that firms were absorbing costs, which is why the consumer index had not moved. The figures replicate; the conclusion does not. The CPI gasoline index rose 24.9% in the single month to 31 March, the largest monthly move in the window and larger than March 2022 — far too recent to be sitting in anyone's margin. The margin compression that is genuinely visible, finished goods over processed materials ex food and energy at −1.37% year on year, is a metals story that ran from October 2025 to January 2026 and has already flattened. For this book the relevant detail is that coal, Indonesia's export, is −2.98% year on year and not in the shock at all.

The labour market has stalled in headcount — but the concentration claim this report built on it does not hold. Payrolls stood at 158,637,000 at 31 March, +0.164% year on year. That is, outside the pandemic, the third-weakest reading in the shipped window — not the 15th percentile, which the 2020 collapse manufactures — and the deceleration has flattened since a December trough of +0.073% rather than decaying monotonically. The revision finding survives: twelve prints summing to +902,000 produced a level rise of 260,000, so 642,000 (71%) was revised away, with the gap negative in twelve of fourteen months, though it has narrowed from 1,092,000 in December.

The concentration is where it fails. Education and health did add 663,000 and leisure and hospitality 176,000, but construction (+57,000), other services (+35,000) and utilities (+4,600) also grew, each faster in percentage terms than the aggregate. And stripping education and health leaves −403,000 — of which 330,000 is a federal headcount cut, down 11.0% year on year. Private payrolls excluding education and health are −161,000, or −0.15%: flat, not contracting. This report said US payrolls ex-healthcare are shrinking by roughly 400,000 a year; that is a public-sector reduction wearing a private-sector label. Aggregate private hours are +0.73% against private headcount of +0.372%, an hours-per-worker wedge of 0.36pp that sits well inside the historical range of that measure.

Fiscal is not the drag report two called it. The three headline numbers replicate exactly — twelve-month outlays +0.63%, receipts +9.35%, deficit narrowing USD 422.4bn — but 59% of that improvement is a single non-cash accounting line, and excluding it outlays ran +4.34%. The financing side points the same way: marketable debt rose USD 2,259bn over the same twelve months, the Fed's return is confined to bills, and foreign coupon buying fell for a third consecutive year to USD 390.7bn.

Two industry facts worth carrying. US residential is a three-year stall with a single-family recession inside it: total starts are flat at 1,368k against 1,369k on a twelve-month average, while single-family fell 7.7% and multifamily rose 21.8%. And heavy trucks are in an outright capex recession that freight volume flatly contradicts — rolling twelve-month sales of 395,271 are the lowest of the entire window, below even the COVID trough of 409,508, while the freight transportation index set a record 139.8 in February. Fleets are moving more goods than ever and refusing to replace equipment.

The transmission to this book remains the long end rather than the policy rate. The ten-year less fed funds spread widened to +0.61pp at 31 March from −0.05pp a year earlier, and thirty-year mortgages backed up to 6.41% from a 6.14% February trough while policy sat frozen. Foreign official custody at the Fed is at the 0th percentile, −8.7% year on year, holding 17.2% of Treasury notes outstanding against 29.8% in December 2019. Reserve managers as a bloc are no longer the marginal buyer of US duration, so if the firm's hedging assumes Treasuries rally in risk-off, that needs stress-testing against the case where they do not.

China

China matters here as the end market for the commodity complex that is the book's largest correlated exposure. Round three challenged both China notes and both required correction.

Property is contracting, but less exceptionally than reported. Real estate development investment fell 11.2% year on year in Q1 2026, widening from −9.9% in Q1 2025, after −17.2% across full-year 2025. Completions at −25.0% were the fastest-falling leg in the quarter, but report two called this the fastest of the cycle and it is not: full-year 2024 printed −27.7%. New starts at 587.7m sqm are 74.1% below 2019, though the pace of decline has narrowed for three consecutive first quarters, from −28.3% to −24.8% to −20.2%. Completions also fell faster than starts by a wider margin in 2024 than now, so Q1 2026 repeats a 2024 pattern rather than opening a new leg. The cleanest signal is prices: 70-city new-build has re-deteriorated for five straight months to −3.4%, with the transaction-implied national average selling price at −6.2%.

Industrial output is being absorbed, and report two said this could not be checked. Industrial value added slowed to +6.1% year to date from +6.5% a year earlier, with the construction chain deepening its contraction — cement −7.1%, crude steel −4.5% to 247.6Mt, construction gross value added −3.8% from +3.1% — against a narrow state-directed equipment impulse, with excavators +39.7% to 112,107 units and rail fixed investment +12.6%. Report two concluded that the archive held no utilisation, orders or inventory series and therefore could not determine whether that output was being absorbed. It does, and it is: the NBS product-sales ratio rose to 93.8% from 93.0%, the first Q1 improvement since 2019; PMI new orders held 51.6; industrial profits accelerated to +15.5% year to date from +0.8%.

The one genuinely alarming Chinese number is about coal, and it was missed. Capacity utilisation in coal mining and washing fell to 66.41% at 31 March from 71.88% a year earlier, the lowest reading outside the 2020 COVID quarter. Together with twelve-month coal import volume at 491.9mt, −9.2% year on year and −9.6% from its February 2025 peak, that is the most direct available read on demand for Indonesian thermal exports, and it points down.

The trade surplus halved for a composition reason. The March goods surplus fell to USD 51.1bn from USD 101.9bn a year earlier as imports jumped 27.8%. That reads as recovering demand until the composition is examined: roughly two thirds of the Q1 import increase is bullion and semiconductors, not raw material volume.

Against copper at the 100th percentile of its own year with speculative positioning at the 94th, the Chinese construction channel does not support the price. But note the correction above: if completions entered their fastest contraction in 2024 rather than now, then the copper demand shock from Chinese construction is not a future event to position for. It has already happened.

Two of these were re-checked at the desk after their agent runs failed, and both hold with caveats worth stating. Every quoted Chinese fixed-investment figure reproduces exactly at 31 March: real estate −10.60, private −2.20, tertiary −1.00, IT and software −6.10, special-purpose equipment −0.40, and coal mining +25.50, which independently confirms the self-sufficiency reading. But the “power, gas and water decelerating from +26.0% to +9.0%” leg is not in this pack at all and is unsupported. And the sequential trend cuts against the framing: five observations earlier, real estate was −14.90, private −4.50 and tertiary −5.30, so all three are improving from worse levels rather than deepening. The genuine deterioration is IT and software, which went from +11.20 to −6.10. “Nothing has replaced property” holds on levels; it should not be read as a worsening trend.

What this means for the book

The first report could not determine whether the three Indonesian positions shared a thesis or were three independent mistakes, and said so. The mortgage data answers it, and the answer survived challenge.

Agung Podomoro and Paramita Bangun Sarana are both exposed to the Indonesian property and construction complex, and that complex has a verified, record-level break in exactly the mass-market segment they sit in: apartment arrears at the highest reading in the entire series, sub-21m² landed at the 100th percentile, and apartment mortgage growth negative after fourteen consecutive months of deceleration, against a premium segment that is clean and improving. These are not two independent mistakes. They are one exposure held twice, in the weakest segment, and losses of −70% and −32% are consistent with the credit data rather than with mispricing.

Two things sharpen it. Foreign investors have been persistently distributing the Indonesian property complex over the last twenty sessions, with LPKR the largest single distribution in the market at −0.34% of its own turnover. And the sector trades at 0.46x book on a 2.4% return on equity, so the market has already marked the segment; there is no catalyst in the credit data for a re-rating while apartment mortgage growth is negative and arrears are still rising.

One correction to an earlier draft of this section. It attributed the property weakness partly to a supply glut financed by a 46% surge in construction credit. That surge is a two-month discontinuity in a series that was flat to down for the preceding twelve months, and it cannot be distinguished from a reclassification. The demand-side and arrears legs stand on their own and do not need it.

The funding data adds a larger question the book has not been asked. If the marginal spread on Indonesian bank lending has halved in five months, if the domestic equity bid has withdrawn to the 12.9th percentile, and if net foreign selling over five months already exceeds the worst full year in the sample, then the relevant risk is not the two property names. It is whether any Indonesian equity exposure should be sized off multiples struck on 24 May.

Round four resolved the coal position, and it took three attempts to get right. Report two implied a broad energy input-cost story. Report three said coal was outside the shock and Indonesian exporters faced no cost pressure. Both were wrong, and the completed picture has three legs, each now verified:

  • Volume is supported, not collapsing. The −9.2% trailing-twelve-month Chinese coal import figure this report leaned on is backward-looking and will keep falling mechanically for another quarter as the weak Q2 2025 base rolls through. The forward-looking same-quarter series reads +1.2% on a shipped-window Q1 record of 116.3mt, alongside Q1 records in iron ore at 314.8mt and crude at 146.9mt. Furnaces and refineries are running at full tilt.
  • Price is recovering. HBA rose 18.3% between March and June with its year-on-year turning from −12.5% to +23.6%, and realised export unit value rose 18.0% over the same February-to-April window as the US fuel spike.
  • Costs are rising hard, and this is the leg the report denied. Pertamina Dex diesel rose 77% between February and April and 103% year on year, the largest move in the series since 2018. Diesel is the primary mining and haulage input, so unit cash costs are climbing into the price recovery.

Net: the long Indonesian coal book is supported on both volume and price and squeezed on margin. That is a materially different position from either of the two this report previously published, and the US coal producer price both rested on is a domestic mine-mouth series with no steam-versus-metallurgical split that carries no read on Indonesia in either direction.

Method, assurance and limits

This pass read the CEIC-style archive already inside the system, which the first pass did not touch: 2,101 Indonesian macro series, 2,061 Indonesian industry series, 3,988 Chinese and 4,252 US. Filtered to series with 2026 observations and at least 24 data points, that produced 84 category packs, joined to the 636-name Indonesian and 169-name global fundamental tables to build fourteen industry packs. Twenty analysts read them independently, then fourteen adversaries were given the same data and told to break the results. Fifty-two agents ran in total across two workflows, consuming roughly 3.9 million tokens.

Assurance is not uniform, and the report marks it. The fourteen Indonesian industry notes were adversarially verified and eleven of seventeen load-bearing claims failed. The six Indonesian macro notes, five US notes and three China notes were not challenged. Given the hit rate on the material that was tested, the untested material should be assumed to contain errors of the same kind until it is put through the same pass. That is the single most important caveat in this document.

Storage. Every heavy extract sits in a local folder outside the repository and outside the database, roughly 19MB of archive packs, industry joins, valuation output and forward indicators. Only the conclusions in this report are written back.

What is still missing. No earnings estimates or revisions anywhere, so nothing here is forward-looking on company fundamentals. US national accounts, personal income and the saving rate are absent, so the US consumption read is proxy-based. China has no utilisation, orders or inventory series, so the equipment-versus-materials divergence cannot be resolved. There is no Japanese, Korean, Taiwanese, European or Indian archive at all. Indonesian GDP itself was not in the extracted set.

Data defects found, including our own. Two files in the Indonesian set disagree about the current stance of monetary policy; the daily series showing the May reversal should be believed. Our own extract script wrote its run date into a field named fundamentals_as_of, which caused four independent analysts to doubt a correct staleness claim; the field has been renamed. Several packs ship a truncated 18-observation recent array beside a 100-observation series, which caused at least two false “first time in the record” claims and is the single most damaging defect found. Two US CBO series presented as distinct concepts carry identical values across all 34 observations. US metadata claims 2016 start dates where arrays begin in 2018, biasing every percentile. A Cleveland Fed series is mis-tagged as a Fed projection, and no projection series exists anywhere in the archive, so no US growth or policy-path forecast can be sourced from it. The Indonesian Energy sector's median price-to-book of 15,556 is corrupted at source.

A correction to this report's own recommendation. Earlier drafts called refreshing the equity fundamentals the single highest-value next action. It remains the most valuable thing to do — the marks are eleven weeks old and predate the policy reversal — but it is not something this system can execute. Nothing in the repository populates the two fundamentals tables: no pipeline module, no scheduled workflow, no API route. They are an external snapshot. The recommendation should read obtain a fundamentals feed, not run the refresh, and until one exists every multiple in this report will keep ageing without any mechanism to update it.
